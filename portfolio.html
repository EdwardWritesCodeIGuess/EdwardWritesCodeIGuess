Career Research — Data Center Network Engineer

20 pts

A Data Center Network Engineer designs, builds, and maintains the physical and logical network infrastructure inside large-scale facilities — routers, switches, fiber runs, and the software that connects them. These are the people who keep cloud services, streaming platforms, and enterprise applications running around the clock.

Honors Reflection

10 pts

The career I'm most interested in is Data Center Network Engineering — specifically, the people responsible for keeping large-scale infrastructure alive. These engineers maintain the routers, switches, fiber runs, and server racks inside facilities that power cloud services, streaming platforms, and enterprise software. It's not a visible role from the outside, but it's one of the most foundational jobs in modern technology. When a data center goes down, the whole internet notices.

This career interests me because I've already been doing a smaller version of it at home. For the past two years I've been building and running a home lab — Proxmox virtualization, VLAN segmentation, Pi-hole DNS security, game server hosting. Every time something breaks at 2 AM, I'm the one who has to figure out why. That's exactly the kind of ownership a real data center engineer has over production systems, just at a different scale. I already think like this; I just want to do it for infrastructure that actually matters to more than just me.

Skills I already have include Linux server administration, IP address planning, VLAN and DNS configuration, hypervisor management with Proxmox, firewall rule writing, basic bash scripting, and technical documentation. I'm comfortable reading logs methodically, troubleshooting systematically rather than randomly, and writing down what I find so it doesn't have to be figured out again.

Skills I need to develop include formal routing protocol knowledge — BGP, OSPF, and IS-IS — along with network automation using Python and Ansible. I also have no experience with enterprise-grade hardware: Cisco Catalyst switches, Juniper routers, and the structured cabling that holds a real data center together. And I need to get better at working within professional change management and ticketing systems, which are central to any production environment.

The portfolio piece that best represents my abilities is the Network Topology Design Document. It shows I don't just use networking tools — I think about infrastructure at a design level, document decisions formally, and treat my home lab the same way a professional would treat an enterprise network. That's the work habit an employer actually cares about.

Researching real job postings changed how I understood this career. The Equinix posting caught me off guard — I didn't realize how physically demanding data center work is at the infrastructure level. Lifting servers, running cable through ceiling conduit, working overnight maintenance windows. It's as much a trade as it is IT work. That makes me more interested, not less, because it means the job has real depth that you can't fake with a certification alone.

To become employable over the next two years, I need to earn CompTIA Network+ and then Cisco CCNA — those two credentials appeared in nearly every job posting I found. I should also build out my GitHub with real automation scripts and configuration examples, and set up a virtual routing lab in GNS3 or EVE-NG on Proxmox so I can practice BGP and OSPF without needing physical Cisco hardware.

My immediate next step is to register for the Network+ exam and start working through the Professor Messer study guide. The knowledge is mostly already there from the home lab work — I just need to formalize it, get it certified, and then push further into the enterprise routing territory that separates a home lab enthusiast from a working network engineer.
